research

How social relationships shape health and well-being in later life—and how later-life transitions reshape those relationships in return.

I study social relationships as both a source and an outcome of health in later life. My work centers on older adults in South Korea and Singapore and combines social network measures with latent class and transition models, dyadic designs, and longitudinal analysis.

Current program

Sociocentric network analysis

My current projects use complete community network data that repeatedly record who names whom among older residents of rural Korean townships. Observing everyone in the community makes it possible to distinguish the ties people report from the nominations they receive—something egocentric surveys cannot do.

Setting Rural Korean townships
Design Whole-community panels
Key distinction Sent and received ties
01

Being chosen and loneliness

Do nominations older adults receive—especially from non-kin—protect against later loneliness beyond the ties they name themselves, and does that differ in advanced old age?

02

Selection or influence

When does similarity between connected villagers reflect choosing similar others, and when does it reflect influence as networks and behavior co-evolve? Stochastic actor-oriented models compare visible behaviors—social participation, drinking, and smoking—with less visible states such as depressive symptoms.

03

From teeth to ties

How does tooth loss change the discussion nominations older adults receive, and what does that mean for later cognitive function?

04

Network trajectories

How do received and sent ties change across survey waves, and how are those long-run trajectories linked to cognitive aging?

05

Cultural holes

What does it mean for well-being when older adults bridge culturally dissimilar people who are not otherwise connected? This project develops culturally weighted measures of brokerage to find out.

Published work

Four foundations

The current program grows from four connected lines of research on networks, health, caregiving, and social capital.

Networks over time

Social network typology

I use latent class and transition analysis to identify older adults' network types and trace movement between them—after widowhood, during the pandemic, and over ordinary time—with consequences from depressive symptoms to mortality. Newly accepted work extends this lens to whether loneliness depends on the status of one's ties, one's own status, and the surrounding region (Journal of Health Psychology, accepted).

  • Getting Lonely and Isolated? Social Networks · 2026
  • Stability and Changes in Social Network Profiles After Widowhood Population, Space and Place · 2026
  • Transitions between Social Network Profiles and All-cause Mortality Social Science & Medicine · 2022
Reciprocal processes

Social engagement and health

This work treats engagement and health as processes that continually shape each other, spanning cognitive function, depressive symptoms, handgrip strength, and oral health. Recent work also maps multimorbidity among older Koreans living alone and links it to healthcare use and unmet needs.

  • Multimorbidity Patterns, Healthcare Utilization, and Unmet Needs Geriatrics & Gerontology International · 2026
  • Oral Health-Related Quality of Life and Social Engagement Journal of Dental Research · 2026
  • Formal Social Engagement, Oral Health, and Depressive Symptoms International Journal of Geriatric Psychiatry · 2025
  • Social Engagement and Handgrip Strength Gerontology · 2024
Care in context

Family caregiving

Studies of families caring for older adults with cognitive impairment show that well-being depends on more than the amount of care: loneliness, task-sharing, mastery, and resilience all shape the caregiving experience.

  • Loneliness in Family Caregiver–Care Recipient Dyads Geriatrics & Gerontology International · 2026
  • Sharing in Caring The Journals of Gerontology, Series B · 2025
  • Stress and Loneliness among Family Caregivers Journal of Aging and Health · 2025
Comparative perspective

Trust and social capital

Cross-national research examines how generalized trust relates to social networks—especially weak ties—and the resources they carry, alongside comparative work on in-group trust and health in East Asia.

  • Lonely by Comparison: Understanding Loneliness Through Network Alter Status, Ego Status, and Regional Status Journal of Health Psychology · 2026 · Accepted
  • Generalized Trust, Social Networks, and Social Resources across 30 Countries International Sociology · 2024
  • In-Group Trust and Self-Rated Health in East Asia International Journal of Comparative Sociology · 2020
